Curious about Islam? You’re in the right place.

Whether you’re a neighbour who has always wondered what goes on inside the mosque, someone quietly reading about Islam, or a new Muslim finding your feet — this page is for you. People of all faiths and none are welcome here, and no one will ever pressure you into anything.

The basics

What Muslims believe

Islam is not as unfamiliar as it may seem. It belongs to the same family of faiths as Judaism and Christianity, and much of what Muslims believe will sound recognisable to anyone raised in Northern Ireland.

At its heart, Islam is about one simple idea: there is one God, who made everything, and our lives are lived in gratitude and answerability to Him. Everything else — the prayers, the fasting, the charity — flows from that.

One God

Muslims worship one God — the creator of everything, with no partners, no images and no intermediaries. The Arabic word for God is Allah, the same word Arabic-speaking Christians use in their Bibles and prayers.

The prophets

Muslims believe God sent messengers to every people throughout history — including Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus, peace be upon them all. Muhammad ﷺ is honoured as the final messenger in that same line.

The Qurʾān

The Qurʾān is Islam’s holy book — for Muslims, the word of God revealed to Muhammad ﷺ in Arabic. It is recited daily in prayer, and many Muslims around the world learn the whole of it by heart.

Practice

The five pillars

Muslim life rests on five simple practices. You will see most of them happening at the masjid on any given week.

الشَّهَادَة

Shahādah — faith

The declaration that there is no god but God, and that Muhammad ﷺ is His messenger — saying it sincerely is what makes a person Muslim.

الصَّلَاة

Ṣalāh — prayer

Five short prayers spread through the day, each a few minutes of standing, bowing and quiet words before God.

الزَّكَاة

Zakāh — charity

An annual gift of 2.5% of one’s savings to those in need — not an optional extra, but a duty owed to the poor.

الصَّوْم

Ṣawm — fasting

During the month of Ramadan, Muslims go without food and drink from dawn to sunset, ending each day with a shared meal.

الحَجّ

Ḥajj — pilgrimage

Once in a lifetime, those with the health and means travel to Makkah for the pilgrimage that gathers Muslims from every nation.

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ

“There is no compulsion in religion.”

Qurʾān · Al-Baqarah 2:256

Come and see

Visiting the masjid

A mosque is not a mysterious place — it is mostly a large carpeted room where people pray, learn and chat. Here is what a first visit looks like, step by step.

  1. Get in touch first if you can. You’re welcome to simply turn up around a prayer time, but a quick call or WhatsApp message means someone will be ready to meet you and show you around.
  2. Shoes come off at the prayer hall. The prayer hall carpet is where people place their foreheads in prayer, so everyone — Muslim or not — leaves their shoes on the racks at the entrance.
  3. You may see people washing. Before praying, Muslims perform wudū — a short wash of the hands, face, arms and feet. It is about coming to prayer clean and composed, and there are dedicated wudū areas for it.
  4. Dress modestly. Ordinary, unrevealing clothing is all that’s asked — whatever you would wear to any place of worship. A headscarf is optional for women visitors.
  5. Watch the prayer if you like. Visitors are welcome to sit quietly at the back of the hall and observe. The prayer itself takes only a few minutes; no one expects you to join in.
  6. Ask anything afterwards. Questions are genuinely welcome — the honest ones most of all. And this being Northern Ireland, you will at some point be offered tea. Resistance is possible, but rarely successful.

No question too small

Honest answers to common questions

These are the questions visitors and enquirers actually ask us. If yours isn’t here, just ask — in person, by phone or on WhatsApp.

Do I have to be religious to visit?

Not at all. Plenty of visitors come out of simple curiosity — a neighbour, a student with a project, someone who walks past every day and finally wants to see inside. You don’t need to believe anything, know anything or do anything except be yourself. No one will try to convert you.

What happens at Friday prayer?

Jumuʿah is the main congregational gathering of the week, held every Friday at 13:00. It begins with a khuṭbah — a short sermon, partly in Arabic and partly in English — followed by a brief prayer performed together in rows. The whole thing is over well within the hour, and visitors are welcome to observe quietly from the back.

How do Muslims view Jesus?

With deep reverence. Muslims believe Jesus — ʿĪsā in Arabic, peace be upon him — was one of God’s greatest messengers, born miraculously to the Virgin Mary. An entire chapter of the Qurʾān is named after Mary (Maryam), and no one can be a Muslim without honouring Jesus. Where Muslims and Christians differ is that Islam holds Jesus to be a prophet of God rather than God himself — a difference we can discuss respectfully over that cup of tea.

What does “Allahu akbar” actually mean?

It means “God is greater” — greater than whatever is occupying you right now. It opens the call to prayer and is repeated throughout ṣalāh, each time marking a movement and a moment to set daily worries aside. For the world’s Muslims it is a phrase of devotion heard dozens of times a day, in prayer, in gratitude and in everyday life.

Are women welcome at the mosque?

Yes. Craigavon Masjid has a dedicated women’s prayer space, and women take part in prayers, classes and community life. Women visitors are equally welcome — modest dress is appreciated, and a headscarf is optional, not required. If you’d feel more comfortable being shown around by a woman, mention it when you get in touch and we’ll arrange it.

What is Ramadan like in Northern Ireland?

Memorable. The Islamic calendar is lunar, so Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year — and at this latitude the difference is dramatic. A midwinter fast might run little more than eight hours, while a midsummer fast in Craigavon can stretch past eighteen, with dawn arriving astonishingly early. The community carries it together: the masjid hosts Ramadan meals, and breaking the fast with others each evening — the ifṭār — is one of the warmest parts of the year.

Will anyone pressure me about religion?

No. The Qurʾān itself says “there is no compulsion in religion”, and we take that seriously. We are always happy to explain what we believe when asked — but faith that is pushed on someone isn’t faith at all. Come with questions, or come just to look around; either is fine.

I think I want to become Muslim — what now?

Take your time, and know that the step itself is simple. Becoming Muslim means saying the shahādah — the declaration of faith — sincerely, ideally with witnesses. It costs nothing, requires no ceremony and no appointment, and can be done the day you walk in. That said, contacting us first helps: we can answer your remaining questions, be ready to welcome you properly, and — just as importantly — stay alongside you afterwards, with one-to-one support, learning at your own pace and a community that looks after its own. You will not be doing this alone.
